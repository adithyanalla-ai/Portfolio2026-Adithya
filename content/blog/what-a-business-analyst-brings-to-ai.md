I've spent my career on both sides of a line that many teams draw firmly: the people who decide what to build, and the people who build it. I started in marketing analytics, spent time as a business analyst and AI engineer at once, and now lead AI engineering. Here is what the analyst side contributes.

## Start from the decision

An engineer's instinct is to ask *what can we build?* An analyst asks *what decision are we trying to improve?* The second question produces better AI systems, because it defines what "good" means before any model exists.

> [!KEY] The analyst's first question
> What decision does this change, who makes it, and how will we know it got better?

Working directly with C-suite leaders on strategic decisions taught me that most AI projects don't fail on accuracy. They fail because nobody agreed on what problem they solved.

## The toolkit, applied to AI

The methods I learned as an analyst turn out to be exactly what AI work needs:

- **A/B testing.** At INT360 I designed tests to validate creative and channel choices with evidence. The same discipline is how you prove an AI feature helps rather than assuming it does.
- **Cohort analysis.** Averages hide what matters. Looking at how different groups behave over time is how you find where a model works and where it quietly doesn't.
- **Dashboards leadership actually opens.** A Power BI dashboard that leaders check every week does more for an AI programme's survival than any model card.

## Measure in the business's units

The numbers I'm asked about most aren't model metrics:

<div class="figures">
  <div><strong>₹1.4Cr+</strong><span>business impact facilitated</span></div>
  <div><strong>3,000+</strong><span>qualified leads generated</span></div>
  <div><strong>20+ hrs</strong><span>of staff time automated each week, across 4 organisations</span></div>
</div>

Each of those came from systems with plenty of technical depth behind them. But they're described in revenue, leads and hours because that's the language the people funding the work use.

## When one person holds both roles

Holding both roles shortens the loop. The same person hears the business problem, frames it as something measurable, builds the system and reports the result. Nothing gets lost in a handover document.

It isn't the only way to structure a team. But if you're building AI inside a business, make sure someone on the team thinks like an analyst, and gets a seat where the decisions are made.

> [!TIP] If you're hiring for this
> Look for people who can explain a model's value without mentioning the model. It's the fastest test I know for whether someone thinks in outcomes.
